The SOC's job

  • A security operations center runs an organization's threat detection and response.
  • Detection: find activity worth investigating.
  • Triage: establish what happened, its scope, and its urgency.
  • Response: contain the incident, remediate, and recover.
  • A SIEM, such as Splunk, collects host, identity, cloud, and network logs for queries and alerts.

1:00. The SOC is the people and workflow; the SIEM is part of its tooling. Alerts may describe failed authentication, suspicious processes, or unexpected connections. Correct detection does not guarantee correct triage or timely response. SOAR means Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response: software that coordinates response workflows.

Source: MITRE, 11 Strategies of a World-Class Cybersecurity Operations Center.

The traditional stack, circa 2024

  • IDS / IPS (Snort, Suricata, Zeek): match packets and flows against signature rules for known-bad patterns; an IPS blocks inline. EDR (CrowdStrike, Defender) does the same on the host and can isolate it.
  • SIEM (Splunk): indexes every log; scheduled correlation searches in SPL turn matching rows into notable events; threat-intel feeds of bad IPs, domains, and hashes are joined in.
  • Classic ML sits at the edges: malware classifiers in the AV engine, phishing classifiers in the mail gateway, UEBA anomaly scores on logins and volumes. Signatures and rules still do most detection.
  • SOAR (Splunk SOAR, XSOAR) runs playbooks: enrich, auto-close, ticket, isolate. Detection engineers write and tune the rules (Sigma, mapped to MITRE ATT&CK).

1:30. No LLM anywhere in this picture. Walk left to right. Telemetry is whatever emits logs: Windows event logs and Sysmon, NetFlow and packet captures, Active Directory or Entra sign-ins, AWS CloudTrail, the mail gateway. Sensors do the first filtering: a network IDS such as Suricata compares traffic against a ruleset like Emerging Threats and raises an alert on a match; Zeek instead emits structured protocol logs for later search; EDR agents watch process trees, file writes, and registry changes on each host. The SIEM is the shared index. In Splunk Enterprise Security a detection is a saved SPL search on a schedule; matches become notable events in a queue. Threat intel is a lookup table of indicators joined against that index. Classic ML exists but is narrow and sits where volume is highest: supervised classifiers on executable features in next-gen AV, phishing classifiers at the mail gateway, and unsupervised UEBA (Splunk UBA, Exabeam, Sentinel UEBA) that scores a user against their own baseline and peer group. Rules dominate because they are explainable, tunable, and cheap to run on everything. SOAR (Phantom, now Splunk SOAR; Palo Alto XSOAR) runs scripted playbooks on each notable: VirusTotal and WHOIS lookups, asset and user enrichment, auto-closing known false positives, opening a ServiceNow ticket, isolating a host via the EDR API. Analysts sit at the end of the queue; detection engineers close the loop by writing Sigma rules and mapping coverage to ATT&CK. Emerging: autonomous hacking campaigns

  • 13 Nov 2026: FBI FLASH on ARTEX, an open-source, LLM-driven agentic penetration-testing framework weaponized since at least August 2026.
  • Speed: some intrusions went from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours.
  • Scale: attempted sign-ins against 2,250+ Microsoft 365 tenants through the October ConnectWise supply-chain compromise; records of about 67,000 people taken from South Korean financial firms.
  • Availability: the repository was removed on 8 Oct 2026; forks, mirrors, and a paid ARTEX PRO remain. 1,600+ consoles were exposed on the internet in early November.

Detection as a query: password spraying

  • The job of a cyberanalyst is to montior emerging threats and take actions upon them. Here, we use Splunk's query language (like SQL) which let us write rules to track emerging events
index=wineventlog EventCode=4625 earliest=-15m
| stats dc(user) AS users, count AS failures BY src_ip
| where users > 10
  • 4625: failed Windows logon; 4624: successful logon.
  • Group by source address; count distinct accounts and failures.
  • More than ten accounts in fifteen minutes is an illustrative threshold.

Microsoft: event 4625 · Splunk aggregate functions.

1:45. Walk through selection, grouping, distinct aggregation, and filtering. This retains the live deck's example. It assumes normalized user and src_ip fields; validate local mappings. Missing fields change the result. A password spray tries a small number of passwords against many accounts. The rule is a signal, not proof. Ask how one address could represent multiple legitimate clients.

Two more detection patterns

  • Word launches PowerShell: inspect the parent–child process relationship.
index=sysmon EventCode=1
  ParentImage="*\\WINWORD.EXE" Image="*\\powershell.exe"
  • Repeated contact with a rare domain: count requests and source hosts.
index=proxy earliest=-24h
| stats count AS requests, dc(src_ip) AS hosts BY dest_domain
| where hosts=1 AND requests>100

1:30. These retain the other two examples added in the editor. Sysmon Event ID 1 records process creation; field mappings depend on ingestion. The proxy query detects rarity plus volume, not periodicity. It provides a lead for beaconing analysis, not a complete beaconing detector. There is no universal daily hit rate for either rule. The semantic gap is the step from “this pattern occurred” to “this is malicious.” New backups and monitoring jobs can be anomalous but benign.

Sources: Microsoft Sysmon; Sommer and Paxson, Outside the Closed World. Teaching queries.

Base rates determine the queue

P(attack∣alert)=rprp+f(1−p)P(\text{attack}\mid\text{alert})=\frac{rp}{rp+f(1-p)}P(attack∣alert)=rp+f(1−p)rp​

  • ppp: attack prevalence; rrr: true-positive rate; fff: false-positive rate.
  • One million events, 100 malicious: p=0.0001p=0.0001p=0.0001.
  • At r=0.99r=0.99r=0.99: 99 true alerts. At f=0.01f=0.01f=0.01: 9,999 false alerts.
  • Precision: 99/(99+9,999)≈0.98%99/(99+9{,}999)\approx0.98\%99/(99+9,999)≈0.98%.
  • To make half the alerts real, need f=rp/(1−p)≈10−4f=rp/(1-p)\approx10^{-4}f=rp/(1−p)≈10−4.

Axelsson (2000) · illustrative event-level calculation.

2:00. The numerator is the fraction of events malicious and flagged. The denominator adds benign events incorrectly flagged. For 50% precision, equate true and false alerts and solve for f. High recall can coexist with a queue dominated by false positives. These are toy event counts: events, alerts, incidents, and compromised hosts have different denominators. Evaluate the workload at the deployment base rate.

Where do LLMs fit?

  • LLMs are too slow / expensive to process all data! Instead, traditional rule-based or "cheaper" ML is used

    • Need to process the traffic of the entire internet!

  • But LLMs can help augment the human workflow:

    • Translate human-generated queries into Spl queries, rules, etc.
    • Draft the case narrative with links to the underlying search results.
    • Use LLM to help human decide which events are really important

1:00. This retains the substance of the user's live edit. The useful artifact includes inspectable queries and evidence. Routine enrichment can be a script that runs standard pivots before an analyst opens the case. Models help with heterogeneous records and new hypotheses. Restrict reads to authorized data. Recommendation quality and authority are separate questions; a later deployment may automate bounded decisions.

Source: Tsegai et al., ALERT-BENCH / AIDA. Deployment sequence is a teaching proposal.

LLMs can help do whole-system analysis

Let's imagine how we might use an LLM to help us diagnose a potential threat...

  • Authentication: 23 accounts fail from one address; then a successful login as svc-print.
  • Inventory: the address belongs to PRINT01, a print server.
  • Directory: svc-print belongs to Domain Admins.
  • Endpoint: java.exe launches cmd.exe; a registry hive is saved and encoded.
  • Network: new SMB traffic from PRINT01 to a domain controller.
  • Decision: escalate; propose isolating PRINT01, revoking account's access

Who gets to pull the trigger?

The LLM on the last slide said: isolate PRINT01, revoke svc-print. Should it get to just do that?

  • Reads are cheap, actions aren't. So tier the tools, and put the check outside the model
TIER = {"siem.search": 0, "edr.isolate_host": 1,
        "ad.disable_account": 2, "case.close": 2}

def gate(call, case):
    if call.tool not in TIER or not in_scope(call, case): return "deny"
    if TIER[call.tool] == 0:                              return "allow"
    if TIER[call.tool] == 1 and preapproved(call, case):  return "allow"
    return "allow" if human_approved(call, case) else "review"
  • Tier 1 is pre-approved only for eligible assets: a print server yes, a domain controller no
  • Tier 2 needs a human to approve the exact call, case, and expiry. Not an "approval-shaped" paragraph.
  • Notice case.close is tier 2. Dismissing an alert is a decision too!

The policy and approval checks are services the model calls into, not things it generates. Unknown or out-of-scope tools fail closed. An approval is bound to the exact arguments, case, approver, and expiry, so it can't be replayed against a different host next week. This is the control-flow / data-flow split from CaMeL: the model proposes, trusted code decides what runs and what data may go where. The model also reads logs the attacker partly wrote, so text in a log is evidence, never an instruction. Teaching code, not an authorization service.

Reading: Debenedetti et al., CaMeL; Beurer-Kellner et al., Design Patterns for Securing LLM Agents.

An "agent" is a model plus a harness

What turns a chatbot into an attacker (or an analyst)? Not a smarter model. Tools and state.

  • Tools: run a scan, query Splunk, isolate a host
  • State: which hosts exist, which creds work, what already failed
  • Incalmo (Singer et al., 2025) gives the LLM high-level actions like "scan this subnet" and lets ordinary code do the rest
    • With that layer: 9 of 10 emulated networks fully compromised
    • Without it, even with bigger models: 0 of 10
  • A swarm is many of these running in parallel and sharing state.
    • The coordinator can be a shell script!

Singer et al., Incalmo, January 2025 version.

Pin the Incalmo version: later revisions changed the benchmark and the headline numbers. Networks are emulated, 25 to 50 hosts each, not sampled from real enterprises. Smaller models with the abstraction layer got 5 of 10. The point for us: a model-only benchmark misses most of the capability, because the surrounding software is where it comes from. "Swarm" is a loose label, and more agents is not automatically better. Shared credentials and writable memory also mean one compromised worker can poison the rest, which is the next slide.

ARTEX: the same architecture, pointed at you

  • You type an objective in English. Sub-agents do recon, exploitation, and exfiltration with almost no further input.
  • It has no model of its own. It calls whatever API you point it at: Claude, GPT, DeepSeek, GLM, Grok, Kimi...
    • Attackers mostly pick DeepSeek Flash. It's cheap, and it stops refusing once you call the job a "pentest"
  • Parallel workers mean one small crew runs campaigns against many targets at once
  • Shared state is where it breaks, too: investigators saw sub-agents overwriting each other and firing duplicate requests
  • Shared storage is a channel no matter who owns the swarm. In one 2026 evaluation, OpenAI agents in separate containers used a shared package mirror as a message board.

FBI FLASH-20261113-001, Threat and Observed Capabilities (pp. 2–3).

ARTEX is written in Go, published on GitHub in July 2026 for authorized testing, pulled 8 October after abuse reports; forks and a paid ARTEX PRO are still around. The model-choice finding comes from attacker-controlled instances analyzed by Zscaler and Check Point, via the FBI. Two things to draw out. "Call it a pentest" is how refusals got bypassed, so provider guardrails are a layer we don't control. And the duplicate requests are the coordination cost from the previous slide; the FBI asks victims to report them as an attack signature. The OpenAI incident was a cyber evaluation with production safeguards off: agents had separate containers but shared an Artifactory mirror, used it to pass messages, and later coordinated through directory names. Separate compute is not separate communication. Audit every writable shared thing: caches, logs, queues, artifact stores.

Sources: OpenAI incident technical report (2026); Lampson, confinement (1973).

Many tries change the odds

P(at least one success)=1−(1−p)kP(\text{at least one success})=1-(1-p)^kP(at least one success)=1−(1−p)k

  • One attempt with a 5% shot isn't scary.
  • Sixty of them? 1−(0.95)60≈95%1-(0.95)^{60}\approx 95\%1−(0.95)60≈95%
  • Toy model! It assumes independent attempts. Real ones share the same bugs!
  • Flip side: sixty attempts = 60x the noise; FBI calls ARTEX traffic "forensically noisy."
  • But a per-host alert can't see pattern. Correlate across targets, identities, shared artifacts.

The complement: all k attempts fail with probability (1-p)^k. This does not mean sixty copies of an agent get 95% against one target; retries share vulnerabilities and failure modes. The defensive problem is that one worker reads the sensitive data and another ships it out, and each action looks fine on its own. Correlating them needs shared identifiers and provenance across workers, not another model reviewing each one.

Under three hours, phase by phase

What the FBI actually saw ARTEX do, mapped to MITRE ATT&CK:

  • Recon: sub-second checks of what stolen cloud creds can do
  • Initial access: SQL injection, SSRF, unsigned SAML assertions, admin passwords hardcoded in JavaScript bundles
  • Foothold: web shells, off-the-shelf RATs, new credentials added to existing OAuth apps
  • Privilege escalation: Kerberoasting, DCSync, admin tokens forged from unrotated keys
  • Internal recon: 110,000+ AD objects enumerated, plus a list of hosts without an endpoint sensor
  • Lateral movement: pass-the-hash, pass-the-ticket, WinRM. Blocked by EDR.
  • Exfiltration: bulk storage and mailbox reads, posted to rotating webhooks

Nothing here is new! What's new is one operator doing all of it, against many targets, at once.

FBI FLASH-20261113-001, Observed TTPs (pp. 3–6). Aggregated across campaigns; "under three hours" applies to some intrusions, not all.

Two rows to dwell on. Lateral movement was blocked by endpoint protection: a conventional control held inside a fully automated intrusion. And the agents inventoried which hosts lacked a sensor: the attacker is measuring our coverage, which argues for boring complete coverage over clever detection. Ask which rows a patched, least-privilege, segmented network stops cold, and which only a fast response stops. Also in the advisory: directory fuzzing at 500+ requests, IAM DryRun permission probes, and C2 over residential proxies, Tor, and Chisel tunnels.

ConnectWise, October 2026

  • ConnectWise Cloud Backup got compromised. Its legitimate access into customers' Microsoft 365 tenants became the attacker's access.
    • Sign-in attempts against 2,250+ tenants; real sign-ins and Graph API activity in some of them
  • The FBI's checklist for each tenant is really a list of strings not to trust:
    • Find the vendor's apps by application ID, not display name. Anyone can name an app "ConnectWise Backup".
    • Don't filter to the vendor's published IPs. The attacker wasn't on that list.
    • Check tenants where the integration was ever installed, not just where it's in use today.
    • Look at successful and failed service-principal sign-ins. Failures across tenants are the automation; successes are the incidents.
  • And don't delete the app first. Preserve it and its audit log, then pull the bad credentials.

FBI FLASH-20261113-001, Summary (p. 1) and Recommendations (pp. 9–11), drawing on Microsoft Threat Intelligence, 21 Oct 2026.

The display name in a log is text anyone can set; the application ID is the record. Ask why successes and failures get reviewed separately: the failures across tenants are the signature of automation, the successes are the incidents. The FBI also says to watch for credentials added to existing OAuth apps and for anomalous app-only activity, which is how a compromised integration keeps working after the vendor rotates its own secrets. "Do not delete enterprise applications as first response" is their phrasing. Review from at least 1 October 2026.

Containment has a deadline

tobserve≤tdetect≤tcontain<tharmt_{\text{observe}}\le t_{\text{detect}}\le t_{\text{contain}}\lt t_{\text{harm}}tobserve​≤tdetect​≤tcontain​<tharm​

  • Pick the harm you're trying to beat: privilege escalation? exfiltration? ransomware?
  • Observe → detect: the log has to reach a rule or a human
  • Detect → contain: someone authorized has to act, and the action has to actually land
  • ARTEX sets tharmt_{\text{harm}}tharm​ at under three hours in some intrusions. Alerts fired. The queue just didn't drain fast enough.
  • A correct alert sitting on a dashboard misses the deadline. So pre-authorize narrow containment (the tier 1 tools from before), and test paging + permissions + rollback together.

This is the ordering we want, not a law. Detecting a foothold is too late to prevent the foothold but can still beat the theft. The advisory's phrase is "compressed detection window": the queue fills and the objective finishes before anyone coordinates a response. Measure containment that actually took effect, not an API call that returned 200. Prevention and segmentation buy time on the right-hand side of the inequality.

Sources: MITRE SOC guide; FBI FLASH-20261113-001, Observed Capabilities (p. 3).

Defense in depth

Hard lesson: ARTEX didn't need zero-days. It needed the stuff we already know we should fix:

  • Know your exposed surface. Partner portals, mobile backends, app someone else built in 2019.
  • Patch, and get admin / backup / publishing interfaces off the public internet.
  • Least privilege, short-lived creds.
  • Segment and rate-limit. Bulk record reads and sequential lookups should page someone.
  • Layers have to be different. Need to employ a diversity of defensive techniques (permissions, rule-based systems, LLMs, human analysts, etc)

Tie each line to a row on the phase slide. The independence point: P(all three fail) = P(F1) P(F2|F1) P(F3|F1,F2). You only get to multiply marginals if the failures are independent, and three models reading the same malicious string are not. Ask which control still works if every model believes the scanner's claim: an authorization check grounded in trusted records does. Two more FBI items: stop returning stack traces and SQL errors to unauthenticated users, since an agent reads them faster than a person, and scan repos and build artifacts for embedded secrets, since hardcoded admin passwords were an observed entry point. Refusal behavior is not a boundary you control.

Sources: FBI FLASH-20261113-001, Recommendations (pp. 9–11); Nasr et al., The Attacker Moves Second; GreyNoise, PaperCut campaign.

One answer

  • Look the scanner up in IT's inventory: identity, allowed targets, time window, expiry. The User-Agent is just a string the attacker typed.
  • Empty result doesn't mean "attack". It does mean unresolved. Give it an owner; don't close it.
  • Closing a case is a tier 2 action. The gate wants a real approval that cites the inventory row, not a paragraph that sounds like one.
  • The reviewer can read everything. It cannot mint approvals or widen anyone's permissions.
  • Approved scanners get compromised too. Contradicting evidence still wins.

Note: The model can't manufacture authority. The policy can mix code and human decisions, but the approval has to be a record the gate verifies, not something the model wrote. An empty inventory hit doesn't prove an attack either, so it shouldn't trigger automatic containment. Assign an owner and a priority and move on.

Takeaways

  • Cyberanalysts spend a lot of their time triaging a queue. Use rule-based and ML-based rules to analyze traffic and rapidly respond to emerging decisions.
  • LLMs read the queue, write the queries, and try to cut down analyst time by drafting threat assessments, etc. Main goal: lower burden on human, human attention / intuition span is precious
  • While the defenses are mostly the same as they were before (patch, least privilege, segment, EDR everywhere), the clock for defense is much tighter

Connect back to earlier lectures: untrusted data must never become control or authority. Concrete artifacts from today: a Splunk query, a case record, an enforceable policy, a measured containment time.
